Living With Compassion

As our course comes to a close, it becomes clear to me how important it is to have a plan to activate our learning together. The following ideas are offered as a way to support you in creating a more wonderful life.

I

Harness the Power of Your Dreams

I have a collection of antique science fiction books. Recently, as I was looking through my collection, I noticed that without exception, everything that was imagined in these books has come to be. From the "Great Flying Machines" to the "Great War Machines", we humans have created everything we have ever imagined. It seems that every human creation starts as an idea, a dream.

As I look at my own life so far, I see how this dynamic applies. Everything I have ever truly dreamed of has come to be. As I notice this rather astounding phenomenon, I realize how important and powerful my dreams have been and continue to be - and how important it is to have them.

II

Practice

As we have discussed in previous weeks, we already have plenty of practice in "habitual" ways of thinking, speaking and being (See Week 9). If we are to make compassionate thinking and living part of our lives, it calls on us to practice and to create new habits, new pathways in our consciousness.

Through practice we will inevitably see results. The reason I have shared the successes and failures that I have experienced in my pursuit of a compassionate life in this course is to remind you that we will always have failure on our path to success - It's part of the journey.

III

Support

The concepts and practices in this course are not typically supported in our common cultures. Many of us are continually encouraged by our environment to think and behave in ways that do not engender the level of compassion and connection we are capable of experiencing. That's a challenge.

We are pioneers. Because of this, finding others to share this with becomes vital. In my experience, those of us who find other "like-hearted" folks to practice with have a more fun-filled, effective and sustainable practice. Finding others to join us on our journey helps us grow and thrive as the interdependent creatures we love to be.

From the first day of my journey, I sought support. To this day, I attend practice groups, workshops and have regular calls with my Empathy Buddies. I don't even want to imagine what my life would be like if I did not have this practice as part of it. As far as I can tell, we all need support.

Finding an Answer

When I was 12 years old, I picked up a "Life Magazine", a coffee table-sized publication known for its groundbreaking photography. Little did I know that what I was about to see in that magazine would change the course of my life.

As I leafed through the oversized pages, I came across what I can only describe as one of the most horrifying things I have ever seen.

I saw, for the first time in my young life, the reality of war. There was a photo essay on a military action that had taken place in Viet Nam. Up to that moment, for me, war was a game we played with make-believe guns. No real blood, no real pain, just a game to be won or lost before we headed home for dinner.

As my eyes scanned the pages, my brain was scurrying like a frightened animal, not knowing if it should keep going or turn and run. As I took in these images, two things happened. First, I understood that war was a real and horrible reality of my life as a human. And second, I had an overwhelming and instant knowledge that these actions were an unnecessary and must be avoidable.

That night, and for several days following, I shared my experience with the "grownups" in my life. They assured me that war was a necessary part of life.

My twelve-year-old mind secretly refused to believe them. And yet, I had no answer as to why that was. I looked and I waited for decades.

In 2001, I read the book Nonviolent Communication: A Language of Life by Marshall Rosenberg. By the time I had finished reading the first few chapters, my wait had ended. In the book, Marshall showed that there are concepts, skills and practices that we can develop and use to connect with each other. So much so that we can learn to transcend violence.

From then on, I knew exactly what I wanted to do. I began my study of Marshall's work with the most experienced and integrated trainers I could find. It wasn't long before I met Marshall and eventually worked with him as his support person here in New York. During that time I found a new friend and it became clear to me that my future held the potential for a very meaningful life, beyond my dreams.

After that, he worked with me to start NYCNVC, we trained as a team at The United Nations, and I was one of the first people to stand in for him at CNVC IIT's. My dream was, in fact, becoming a reality.

By 2002, I had sold my engineering business, sold my home, and moved to New York City to put The New York Center for Nonviolent Communication (www.nycnvc.org) in "high gear".

By 2004, in the process of growing this organization and my practice, I had spent every dollar I had. Most everyone was convinced I had lost my mind, with the exception of my parents and my dear friend Nellie (to name the ones I hold the most appreciation for). And maybe I did lose my mind - and found my heart, my dream. I was clear. I was willing to live in a cardboard box if need be, but I could not imagine doing anything besides sharing this work.

I remember one day in particular that year. It was the first Monday of the month and I was especially worried because I was dealing with the reality of my net worth going from six figures to negative six hundred dollars.

That night, I gave my "First Mondays" free introductory workshop, as I had on the first Monday of every month. Afterwards, a woman from a local community service organization asked if she could hire me to teach her and her colleagues. My first paid engagement. I said yes.

The next day (within 14 hours of the first invitation), I got a call from a member of the Communications Coordination Committee for the United Nations. They, too, wanted to hire me to teach. My dream was becoming a sustainable reality.

Twenty-four years later, on a cold sunny Wednesday morning, still in the comfort of my home, I sit down at my computer once again, to put the finishing touches on the last installment of a course I have wanted to teach since I was twelve. Thank you for sharing this moment with me.

With gratitude, humility and love,

Thom
