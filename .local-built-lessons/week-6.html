"Hidden Judgments"

So far during the course, many of us have developed the skill of translating our judgments into an awareness of needs (or at least we are beginning to - *warm smile*). This will be a recurring and vital practice in our journey to having more compassion in our lives.

So it follows that the more we have the skill of identifying judgments, the more opportunity we have to increase our awareness of needs and therefore, our experience of compassion.

This week I would like to share ways to increase our compassion by noticing "hidden judgments": The first way, distinguishing observations from judgments and the second, noticing "non-feeling words".

Distinguishing "Observations" from "Judgments"

— Marshall Rosenberg, Friend, Mentor, Teacher, Poet

As we have discussed in earlier weeks, it can be disconnecting to speak in terms of judgment. That said, most of us still struggle to fully notice our judgments. By learning to think and speak in ways that include more observation and less judgment, we can increase the amount of connection we are likely to experience when we are talking to others (and ourselves).

In the "Practice(s)" section this week, there is an exercise that helps us distinguish these two modes of communicating (judging vs. observing). This, in turn, gives us more choice about how we speak and what we experience as a result. FYI, this exercise can be a challenging endeavor.

Distinguishing "Non-Feeling Words"

Non-feeling words are words that we use as if they are feelings, although they are not really feelings. In fact, they can be seen as judgments disguised in "feeling language". Although we have touched on this topic in earlier weeks, it can take some real intention to develop our ability to notice and translate "non-feeling words" and the thoughts that go with them.

For example, I may say, "I feel excluded." Although, if you think about it, "excluded" is something that happens (or happened). It is not actually a feeling.

That said, it doesn't mean that if someone says they're "feeling excluded", that they are not having feelings (or needs). It just means, they are not included in the words (and likely consciousness).

By translating (or connecting) these words to the feelings and needs beneath them, we can experience more connection, understanding and compassion. For example, in the case of "excluded", I can imagine that a person who is saying that, might actually feel frustrated, lonely or scared and would like to experience more connection, consideration, belonging, or community. When I think about these feelings and needs (and not the word "excluded"), I am more likely to experience more self-connection and a more compassionate understanding of what I'm (or others are) going through.

PRACTICAL NOTE: Many of us, when we become aware of non-feeling words, are tempted to "correct" people who use them. We may even say things like "You know, that's not actually a feeling". As it turns out, that rarely ends up being a connecting experience. I have learned (the hard way) to simply translate or unpack "non-feeling" words inside my own head to create my own sense of compassionate understanding.

I have found this practice of seeing the feelings and needs inside "non-feeling" words to be particularly powerful (even transformational), especially when I notice and translate my own use of these words.

In the "Practice(s)" section this week, there is an exercise that helps us learn the "unpacking process" when it comes to some of these words.

The Pitfall of Non-Feeling Words

When I first started my journey to needs awareness, I was attending a workshop in upstate New York. On Saturday morning, I made arrangements to join my (then) partner during the lunch break. Just after noon, as the participants were gathering in the dining area, I spotted her, seated at one of the far tables. I walked over to sit down and join her, as planned.

She and another participant, Beth, were engaged in a rather animated discussion. As I approached, they both looked up and immediately went back to their conversation.

As I waited for my partner to make room for me at the table, Beth said. “Oh hey Thom. Change of plans - were busy here, we’ve decided to have lunch, just the two of us." My partner smiled and nodded in agreement.

Stunned by this remark, I wandered away from the table, miffed by the sudden, undiscussed change of plans.

I found a new table on my own and attempted to self-empathize and get my center back. Without my feelings list handy, I decided to wing it. I kept thinking, I was feeling “excluded”.

Unlike other times, when I used my feelings (and needs) list, this time I felt exactly no sense of self-connection. All I could focus on was that I wanted them to behave differently and not exclude me. I sat, confused and disconnected, not understanding why my “self-empathy” was so unproductive. I was stuck with one thought - they shouldn’t have excluded me. And more than that, I was drawing a blank, when it came to what I needed, other than, “needing” them to behave differently.

I was becoming more and more upset with this “intruder”, Beth, and increasingly angry with my partner for agreeing to exclude me.

As I was staring down at my improvised notes on my napkin, out of the corner of my eye, I noticed my friend Lisa, standing nearby, looking my way with, what I took as a look of concern. She spoke quietly, “Can I join you?”

Noticing a friendly face I replied, “Of course.”

“Are you OK?”

“Not really.”

I explained what happened and shared my process. After a bit of silence, Lisa asked, “Are you feeling confused, maybe frustrated?" I checked in with myself and indeed those feelings were quite alive in me. After a rich silence, she spoke again. "Are you wanting to experience more understanding... or consideration?" Those words were so meaningful. In that moment, as I listened, I was noticing these needs actually being met as she spoke. In that new moment, I realized that it was not just the behavior that caused my experience - it was the needs that their behavior reminded about - how much I value consideration... then trust popped up too, then partnership.

This distinction between wanting someone to behave differently versus making contact with my feelings, and then needs was a life-changer. I no longer "needed" them to change, I could yearn to experience consideration in my life, and pursue that - not in the form of someone's particular behavior, but as an energy (or experience) of life, one I might find in a thousand different ways. I have come to call this "needs liberation".

I laughed out loud, as I thought,

"the people who remind us of our needs, are rarely the people who are meeting them"

In that moment I had discovered a powerful key to making my life more wonderful, even as the actions of those around me were stimulating pain in me. A lesson that, to this day, changes how I live every day of my life.

If it weren’t for this subtle, yet incredibly powerful distinction between feeling and non-feeling words, I might have missed it.

1 "Shifting towards Compassion"

Continue working with this exercise (The Exercise). This time, start using observations in addition to quotes, such as actions. You can also work with your own judgment thoughts by writing the words "I'm telling myself ____". For example "I'm telling myself Chris is selfish".

2 Experiencing Judgments versus Needs

Journal

Write down 4 judgments you have about your own needs (for example, "I don't deserve to have them", "they are a sign of weakness" - take your time). Next, view or print a copy of the needs list. Then, choose your top 5 favorite needs (for that moment) and write them down. Scan and ponder your lists. Notice how you feel as you "take in" these two different lists. You may want to share this on one of our Community Forums.
